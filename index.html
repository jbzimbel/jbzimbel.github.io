Jacob Bradley Zimbelman

Doctoral Candidate & Instructor in Philosophy

Saint Louis University

I work mainly at the intersection of epistemology, practical ethics, and political theory. My dissertation is on the rationality of political suspicion. I'm currently interested in what implications the rise of AI in mass media and in governance might have for the rationality of a public's trust in its authorities and institutions.

At SLU, I've taught over 400 students across 11 semesters. I mainly teach two courses—Ultimate Questions: The Examined Life and Ethics—which are core requirements for most students. It's a very cool job.

Research & Works in Progress

Throughout my work, I explore various ways in which ideas within ethics, epistemology, action theory, and philosophy of mind are interconnected. Taken together, my research falls into three main areas.

Political Epistemology

My dissertation, Reasonable Suspicion: Conspiracy Theories, Official Stories, and the Limits of Trust investigates a vexing question. When is suspicion suspicion of others warranted?

Making progress on this question can help us see the relationship between practical and epistemic reasons in a new light—and it affords some insight into how we can best address the rise of conspiracy theories in our own communities.

I given an account of conspiracy theories and official stories, defend a neglected common-sense account of the foundations for trust, and propose conditions under which a default suspicion toward authorities and institutions is more reasonable than a default trust.

Rationality, Agency, & How to Live

Active Papers:

  • "The Benefits of the Benefit of the Doubt" (Under Review): I consider how we should break ties between equally reasonable interpretations of others. I argue that, when epistemic considerations are indecisive, we should assess the most promising interpretations based on how good they make others out to be, and we should act on the ones that make them out to be the best by our own lights.

  • "Akratic Agency": I work toward a comprehensive understanding of agency which can accommodate our complex intuitions about weak-willed agents (roughly, agents who don’t behave how they would like) and their actions.

Perception & Reality

Active Papers:

  • "Perspectival Panexperientialism: A Hands-On Metaphysics of Perception": I consider what the world must be like if we assume a robust direct realism about perception, according to which the world is literally present in our experience. I build upon ideas found in the philosophy of Peter Unger and George Berkeley to show how we can make sense of robust direct realism, without resorting to idealism, in a way that accounts for discrepancies in our experiences of the world.

  • "A Walk in the Woods: Metaphor and the Foundations of Epistemology" (Under Review): I build on recent work concerning metaphor to make a novel case for the value of metaphorical concepts: thinking with metaphors allows for a structural isomorphism between our concepts and complex, unified phenomena. I argue that metaphorical thinking finds a perceptual analogue in rational intuition, drawing on ideas from Pseudo-Dionysius, Henri Bergson, and recent work on cognitive phenomenology.
